2 · Why three: the natural-path condition — Eq 3.5, a = 2

The paper fixes its constant $a$ by requiring the circular orbit to be the natural (force-free) path. Slide $a$ off 2 and watch the books refuse to close — the required speed and the actual orbital speed disagree, and the orbit destabilizes. At $a=2$: total coefficient $1+a=3$ — one part relative rate, two parts closed-path geometry, the same $(v/c)^2$ anholonomy that gives Thomas's cap and the geodetic precession in the fixed ratio $1:3:6$.

In the source paper the constant a is fixed by requiring that circular orbits be “geodesics” — the extant record's stage language. Read natively: the circular orbit is the force-free path (a free body rides its natural orbit weightless — the straight line through a void was never the natural motion), and carrying the time-cone around that closed path adds the path's own turning to the velocity term. This is the same one \((v/c)^2\) anholonomy that gives the Thomas cap, the geodetic precession, and the perihelion advance in the fixed ratio \(1:3:6\) (\(\pi : 3\pi : 6\pi\)). Only a = 2 closes those books — the total \(1+a=3\) reads one part relative rate, two parts closed-path geometry — the same number General Relativity reaches through curved-spacetime language.

In an elliptical orbit, time dilation varies continuously. The proper time integral (2.12) captures this — clocks tick slowest at periapsis (closest approach) and fastest at apoapsis (farthest point). This is a direct consequence of the combined gravitational and kinematic time dilation described in POAMS. The vis-viva equation $v^2 = GM\!\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{a}\right)$ determines the speed at each point, while Kepler's second law ensures the orbiter sweeps equal areas in equal times, moving fastest at periapsis and slowest at apoapsis.

4 · The advance of the orbit — Eq 4.6 and Mercury's 43″

Carry the varying clock rate into the orbit equation and a cubic term $2\mathcal{M}u^3$ appears — too small to change the ellipse's shape, large enough that the ellipse never quite closes: the axis advances. For Mercury that advance is the famous measured 43″ per century, the number that made Einstein's name — here read from the time book, no field equations consulted. About This Exhibit

POAMS (the Pope–Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis) derives perihelion precession from orbital time dilation — without invoking Einstein's field equations or curved spacetime geometry.

The key insight: a gravitational potential alters the rate of local clocks, modifying the effective orbital equation. The resulting $2\mathcal{M}u^3$ correction term is identical in form to the GR prediction, producing the same 43 arcseconds/century for Mercury.

Move the precession slider to zero to see a closed Newtonian ellipse, then increase it to watch the perihelion advance — the hallmark signature that confirmed general relativity, here derived from first principles in POAMS.

Explanation

GPS satellites must correct for time dilation effects. POAMS predicts the same corrections as General Relativity — derived purely from conservation of angular momentum and the time dilation formula, without Einstein's field equations.

Eq 2.9 (velocity only):   $\tau = \sqrt{1 - \frac{GM}{rc^2}}\; t$

Eq 3.6 (full orbital):   $\tau = \sqrt{1 - \frac{3GM}{rc^2}}\; t$

The factor of 3 in Eq 3.6 arises because orbital motion contributes both kinetic and gravitational time dilation — linked through angular momentum conservation. For a circular orbit, $v^2 = GM/r$, so the velocity term adds another $GM/rc^2$ on top of the gravitational $2GM/rc^2$.

For GPS at 26,571 km, the satellite clock runs ~38 μs/day fast relative to ground. Without correction, positioning errors grow by ~11.4 km/day.
