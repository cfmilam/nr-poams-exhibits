Correlation, memory, and the next exchange

An exploration extending the work of N. Vivian “Viv” Pope and Dr. Anthony D. Osborne, through an explicitly adopted classical model.

Two systems can show the same local readings, carry exactly the same energy and angular momentum, and still exchange energy differently. The missing information is not another substance. It is how their states are related.

1. Same accounts. Different arrangement.

Picture two outer rotors, each connected to a central rotor. Prepare the outer displacements with either the same sign or opposite signs. Repeat each preparation with balanced sign reversals and opposing initial momenta. Every individual rotor and every individual connection then has the same initial state distribution in both ensembles. Every trial has the same total energy and zero total angular momentum.

What differs is the joint correlation between the outer displacements. The central rotor responds to both connections together. Equal separate readings do not fix their combined response.

2. The construction

Let s and r be independent, equally likely signs. The ensemble label η is +1 for A and −1 for B. The three angular displacements and angular momenta are:

q = (s a, η s b, 0)   p = r(P, −P, 0)

Each ensemble contains four equally weighted states. The adopted energy is kinetic energy plus the energy of the two linear torsional connections:

H = Σ pᵢ²/(2Iᵢ) + k₁(q₁ − q₃)²/2 + k₂(q₂ − q₃)²/2

Every prepared state therefore has the same H = P²/(2I₁) + P²/(2I₂) + k₁a²/2 + k₂b²/2. Changing η changes none of these terms. It changes the central initial torque, s(k₁a + ηk₂b), whose squared magnitude controls the early central energy gain.

⟨K₃⟩A − ⟨K₃⟩B = (2k₁k₂ab/I₃)t² + O(t⁴)

Here I = (1, 2, 4), k = (0.7, 1.3), a = 0.2, b = 0.3 and P = 1. The leading coefficient is 0.0273. Energy is redistributed, not created. These are finite prepared ensembles, not equilibrium thermal ensembles.

3. Why this is memory

The full model retains all three coordinates and momenta. If a reduced description drops a predictive coordinate, eliminating it can leave a response that depends on prior states. Alternatively, keep the missing state explicitly. Memory in the reduced description and additional state in the fuller description are two ways to account for the same dynamics.

Our ensemble comparison makes the point more sharply: even matching all the stated local distributions does not specify the joint preparation. A predictor given those marginals alone cannot distinguish A from B. A predictor given their correlation can. This is not a claim that every predictor using local measurement histories must fail; a sufficiently informative history may reconstruct missing state.

Conservation tells us what balances. A sufficient state description tells us what happens next.

One seeded run per scenario; no coverage or false-positive-rate survey. The noisy run checks mean energy equivalence, not exact equality of every trial. Its controls work under stipulated independent calibration assumptions. It tests the protocol, not nature.

5. What is second sound?

Second sound is a propagating temperature-and-entropy disturbance, rather than ordinary diffusive thermal spreading. Conventional literature describes this as a “temperature wave.” Ordinary sound is primarily a pressure-and-density disturbance; second sound is primarily thermal. It is not an echo or a second audible tone.

The name describes a macroscopic response. It need not introduce a material carrier or a wavelength premise into our model. Research on graphite reports such thermal responses under particular preparation and measurement conditions; the instrument response and thermal interpretation must still be distinguished. Huberman and colleagues, graphite experiment; Sidorenkov and colleagues, entropy response in a superfluid.

Our three-rotor result does not establish second sound. A moving or oscillating energy reading alone is insufficient. A thermal application must independently identify the relevant material states, calibrate the temperature readout, and distinguish the proposed response from coherent mechanical motion, direct ballistic transport and instrument artifacts.

6. From retained correlations to useful control

The next construction is a three-coordinate torsional demonstrator: prepare the two correlation signs, read all three motions, and predict the receiver response from independently calibrated inertias and couplings. Include support exchange, preparation work and unwanted connections. An eventual thermal stage adds an independent thermometer and receiver-energy measurement.

The technological opportunity is correlation-aware sensing and exchange control: learning which joint states must be retained to predict a response that separate readings cannot fix. The present result supplies a concrete test, not yet a material heat-transport law.
