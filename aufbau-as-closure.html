1 · The Question

Why does the 4s mode fill before 3d? Why do the periods run 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32? Chemistry runs on the Madelung rule: modes \((n,l)\) fill in order of increasing \(n+l\), and for equal \(n+l\), increasing \(n\). The rule builds the periodic table (see The Elements and The Atom as Organized Phase, which carry it as an honest boundary) — and in the standard account it is an orphan. Quantum mechanics assumes it. Löwdin (1969) made the scandal formal — "it is perhaps remarkable that, in axiomatic quantum theory, the simple energy rule has not yet been derived from first principles" — and the challenge stands open: the one high-profile claimed solution (Allen & Knight 2002) was rebutted (Scerri 2006). Section 7 recites the full record.

This exhibit reads the rule as closure geometry — and then computes it.

2 · The Dictionary: Three Ways an Orbit Can Come Home

Take any circulation held by a central ledger and ask one question: through how much angle does it sweep between successive closest approaches? Express the answer as the commensurability \( k = \omega_\varphi / \omega_r \) — revolutions per radial period. Semiclassically, modes with \(n_r\) radial quanta and \(l\) winding quanta group into families of equal \( n_r + k\,l \). Three anchors, none fitted:

Bare eye (Coulomb), \(k=1\). One turn, home. The ellipse closes after \(2\pi\) — a radial quantum and a winding quantum cost the same. This is hydrogen's \(n\)-degeneracy.
Oscillator, \(k=\tfrac12\). Pericenter every half turn; families \(2n_r+l\).
Self-dual ledger, \(k=2\). Two turns — 720° — then home, exactly. Demkov–Ostrovsky (1972) proved every zero-energy orbit of this potential closes after two revolutions (\(T_r = 2T_\varphi\)). Families \(n_r+2l = n+l-1\): the Madelung families.
The real census (Thomas–Fermi), \(k\approx1.9\). Two turns and a near-return. It never closes — and the shortfall below 2, mode by mode, turns out to be the filling order.

Live integrations of the four ledgers (RK4, this page). The first three close exactly, by theorem; the fourth precesses forever — watch the pericenter walk. Note where the eye sits: at the focus for the Coulomb ledger, at the center for the oscillator.

3 · The Razor: the Whole Rule Is One Number

Rank modes by the family label \( c = n_r + k\,l \) and scan \(k\) against the empirical 19-subshell sequence (1s … 7p, all 118 elements). The result is an exact equivalence — both clauses of the Madelung rule at once:

\[ \text{full Madelung order} \iff k \in \left(\tfrac{5}{3},\; 2\right) \]

with the tie-break clause ("lower \(n\) first") exactly the statement \(k < 2\) strict. The window edges are analytic: \(5/3\) from the 4f/6s crossing, \(2\) from 3d/4p and 7p/8s. Demanding the standard continuation (8s before 5g) tightens the window to \((7/4,\,2)\). So the question "why Madelung?" becomes one number: why does the growth frontier hold \(k\) just under 2 — almost self-dual, never quite?

4 · The Frontier Principle (the Normal-Realist License)

Standard mechanics has no reason to care whether a classical zero-energy orbit closes. Normal Realism does — closure is the ontology. There is one substance, angular momentum; turning; a quantum is a completed turn (\( \oint d\varphi = 2\pi n \)); a fractional turn is not a smaller thing but no thing, because it does not close and cannot persist.

An atom grows by filing one more quantum into its census, and it files at the frontier — the zero-binding edge, where the newcomer is marginally captured. For the newcomer to exist there, its circulation must close. So the geometry of return at \(E=0\) is not a curiosity: it is the admission criterion for the census. That is the license for everything computed below.

Translation, stated once: the "potential" \( V(r) = -Z\chi(r)/r \) in the computation is the mechanistic representation of a kinematic fact — the net interior circulation the frontier quantum's books must balance against. Closed interior shells are exactly isotropic (Unsöld), so from outside they read as a net reduction: "screening" is census subtraction. No force crosses a gap anywhere on this page; the map has forces, the territory has bookkeeping.

7 · The Full History: a Rule in Search of Its Reason

The claim of this page — that the build-up order is frontier closure geometry — arrives at the end of a ninety-eight-year record. The record is worth reciting in full, because every ingredient was found, published, and set aside; what was missing was never data, and never mathematics — it was a reason the mathematics should be about closure.

The record

  • 1869 — Mendeleev: the periods themselves — recurrence as an empirical law, cause unknown. The table precedes every theory of it.
  • 1928 — Fermi: the statistical atom, applied to the periodic system in its first year. The \(\nu(l,Z)\) curves give the delayed onset of "d and f electrons" — the block architecture as a critical-capture statement in the self-consistent field (Z. Physik 48:73). §5's threshold table is this result, re-evaluated.
  • 1928–1930 — Janet builds the left-step table, each row one value of \(n+l\) — the rule as pure arrangement, before anyone states it as physics. He "corrects" real configurations to fit the pattern; the discrepancies were real (they are §6's anomaly storms). Karapetoff (1930) publishes the ordering rule in functional form.
  • 1936 — Madelung states the \((n+l, n)\) rule as the textbook mnemonic that now carries his name; Wiswesser (1945) gives it a two-part functional form. A rule everyone uses, no one derives.
  • 1952 — Jensen & Luttinger tabulate the Thomas–Fermi first-appearance charges for d, f, and g windings (Phys. Rev. 86:907); Oliphant (1956) states the capture criterion explicitly — \( \max_x[(3\pi Z/4)^{1/3} x\,\varphi(x)] > (l+\tfrac12)^2 \) — in the Thomas–Fermi–Dirac atom (Phys. Rev. 104:954), with the "integrand-positive" formulation traced through Ivanenko & Larin (1953/55).
  • 1962/1968 — Klechkovskii systematizes the \((n+l)\)-group filling and argues its Thomas–Fermi plausibility — in the Russian literature the rule bears his name. Heuristic, honestly labeled as such.
  • 1969 — Löwdin makes the embarrassment formal (Int. J. Quantum Chem. 3:331): "it is perhaps remarkable that, in axiomatic quantum theory, the simple energy rule has not yet been derived from first principles." The Löwdin challenge.
  • 1971/72 — Demkov & Ostrovsky (Sov. Phys. JETP 35:66) reverse-engineer the potential \( U = -2v/[rR(r+R)^2] \) whose zero-energy orbits all close after exactly two turns (\(T_r = 2T_\varphi\) — 720°), so that bound thresholds group exactly by \(N=n+l\), emerging at \( v_N = \tfrac14 R^3 N(N+1) \) — thresholds that track the real period openings. They note — their word, remarkable — that only this member of the focusing family carries a Coulomb singularity at the origin, and that off the frontier (\(E<0\)) "an additional precession appears, and the additional degeneracy is lifted." The geometry of this page, complete, in 1972 — as a postulated potential. The mainstream correctly declines to count a postulate as a derivation, and the result sits as a curiosity for 54 years.
  • 1979–1983 — Wong argues a Thomas–Fermi justification (J. Chem. Educ. 56:714), corrected in 1983; Ostrovsky (1981) extends the model's dynamical symmetry; Kitagawara & Barut (1983) prove the Demkov–Ostrovsky degeneracy is not a finite-dimensional Lie-algebra representation — the clean group-theoretic reading of \(n+l\) does not exist. The structure is stranger than the hydrogen SO(4) story.
  • 1996 — Melrose & Scerri (J. Chem. Educ. 73:498) demonstrate the textbook "4s lies below 3d" explanation is simply false — in Sc–Zn the 3d eigenvalue lies below 4s; the filling order is a total-ledger fact, not an orbital-eigenvalue fact. The most-taught account of the rule is an invention.
  • 2002–2006 — Allen & Knight claim the Löwdin challenge answered (Int. J. Quantum Chem. 90:80); Scerri (2006) rebuts it as circular. The challenge stands.
  • 2010 — Schwarz (J. Chem. Educ. 87:444) gives the modern honest account: d-orbital collapse, differential screening, total-energy vs orbital-energy — the rule as an emergent approximation whose exceptions are structure. §6's tie table is this account, computed.
  • 2011 — Pyykkö (PCCP 13:161): Dirac–Fock calculations scramble the rule beyond Z ≈ 120 — the boundary where relativistic rate bookkeeping overtakes the non-relativistic frontier. The rule has an edge, and it is known.
  • 2012 — Makowski (Phys. Rev. A 86:042117): quantum zero-energy wave packets of the Demkov–Ostrovsky family follow the classical closed orbits — the frontier correspondence is not a semiclassical wish; it is verified quantum mechanics.
  • 2019 — Kholodenko & Kauffman (arXiv:1906.05278) derive Madelung regularities from exactly closed orbits in a Bertrand spacetime with Tietz's analytic potential — the closed-orbit instinct again, again in a model where closure is exact by construction.
  • 2024 — Solovej et al. (arXiv:2406.19839) prove the Thomas–Fermi mean-field atom is rigorously periodic in \(Z^{1/3}\) — periodicity from self-consistency, no ordering read-off. The mathematics keeps circling the same object.
  • 2026 — this page: the small step the record left open — evaluate the closure geometry in the real self-consistent screening function, no model, no knobs. The frontier holds \(k\) just below 2 everywhere; the Madelung families are almost exact; the shortfall supplies the tie-break and the anomaly sites; Fermi's thresholds supply the blocks. The rule is not an orphan — it is what a self-organized census's frontier geometry looks like from inside.

"But we can compute atoms without any of this" — the conventional reply, given its due. Fairly heard, it is strong: Hartree–Fock and Dirac–Fock reproduce ground configurations case by case, exceptions included; Schwarz's account of the d-block is quantitative and correct; nothing in chemistry waits on a derivation of Madelung's rule. And the rule itself is soft — some twenty exceptions — so perhaps, as Scerri has argued, there is nothing lawlike there to derive.

Fairly answered: computing is not deriving — a million converged self-consistent runs contain the rule the way a million tide tables contain the moon. The regularity across 118 elements — two clauses, period doubling, anomaly clusters in fixed locations — is either structure or coincidence, and this page computes it as structure: one geometric number, evaluated in the same self-consistent field the conventional account trusts, yields the order, the blocks, the tie-break direction, and the anomaly sites at its own declared ties. The exceptions are not evidence against a law; they are the near-degeneracies the law predicts. What remained underivable for a century was never the arithmetic — it was the standing of the question. You must first believe closure is physical to ask what the frontier's closure geometry is.
